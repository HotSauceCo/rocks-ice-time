Prologue

Why the Earth Keeps Secrets in Stone

Every rock is a time capsule. Every glacier is a slow, patient sculptor. Together they have rewritten continents, lowered sea levels, and left polished striations that still whisper of ancient cold.

“The mountains are the bones of the Earth, and the glaciers are her memory of ice.”

The Beginning

Hadean & Archean Eons

4.6 – 2.5 billion years ago

Before life had a foothold, Earth was a restless furnace of molten rock and relentless bombardment. The first continental crust began to form — dense, dark, and enduring.

No glaciers yet. The planet was far too hot. But the seeds of everything that would later freeze, fracture, and flow were already crystallizing in the primordial magma oceans.

🪨 Oldest Rocks

Acasta Gneiss (Canada) and Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone — fragments older than 4 billion years.

🌋 Early Crust

Komatiites and tonalites — ultra-hot volcanic rocks that no longer erupt today.

First Great Freezes

Proterozoic Eon

2.5 billion – 541 million years ago

Oxygen rose. Continents grew. And for the first time, Earth experienced true global glaciation — the “Snowball Earth” episodes.

Ice sheets may have reached the equator. When they finally melted, the planet awoke into the Cambrian explosion. Cap carbonates still record the sudden warmth that followed.

❄️ Snowball Earth

Huronian (~2.4 Ga) and Cryogenian (~720–635 Ma) glaciations — possibly planet-wide ice.

🧊 Tillites & Dropstones

Ancient glacial deposits frozen in stone, found from Australia to Namibia.

Tillite Diamictite Cap Carbonate Stromatolite

Life Takes the Stage

Paleozoic Era

541 – 252 million years ago

Seas flooded continents. Trilobites, corals, and early fish flourished. Mountain ranges rose as continents collided — the Appalachians, the Caledonides, the Urals.

Near the end of the era, the late Paleozoic Ice Age gripped Gondwana. Massive ice sheets left striated pavements and glacial valleys across what is now Africa, India, Australia, and South America.

🏔️ Mountain Building

Alleghanian orogeny welded North America and Africa into the supercontinent Pangaea.

❄️ Late Paleozoic Ice Age

Carboniferous–Permian glaciation. Coal swamps grew between glacial advances.

Age of Reptiles & Warm Seas

Mesozoic Era

252 – 66 million years ago

Pangaea broke apart. Dinosaurs ruled the land. The climate was largely greenhouse — warm, with high sea levels and little permanent ice at the poles.

Yet even in this warm world, there were cooler intervals and possible small polar ice caps. The rocks of this time — chalks, sandstones, and volcanic sequences — still dominate many coastlines and mountain belts.

🦕 Warm Climate

Little evidence of major continental ice sheets. High CO₂ kept the planet toasty.

🪨 Iconic Rocks

Chalk (Cretaceous), Navajo Sandstone, Deccan Traps — the geological signature of a restless planet.

Chalk Sandstone Basalt Limestone

The Ice Returns

Cenozoic Era

66 million years ago – Present

After the dinosaurs vanished, mammals rose and the climate slowly cooled. Antarctica drifted over the South Pole and acquired its ice sheet. Later, the Northern Hemisphere joined the freeze.

The Quaternary Period — the last 2.6 million years — has been defined by repeated glacial–interglacial cycles. Ice sheets advanced and retreated across North America and Eurasia, carving the Great Lakes, fjords, and countless U-shaped valleys.

🧊 Antarctic Ice Sheet

Formed ~34 million years ago. Still the largest single piece of ice on Earth.

🌊 Northern Glaciations

Laurentide and Scandinavian ice sheets reached their maximum only ~20,000 years ago.

The Heart of the Matter

Ice Ages & the Language of Glaciers

Glaciers are not static. They flow, scrape, polish, pluck, and deposit. They leave behind a vocabulary written in the landscape:

Cirques & Aretes Bowl-shaped hollows and knife-edge ridges carved high in mountains.
U-shaped Valleys & Fjords The classic glacial signature — steep walls, wide floors, and drowned by rising seas.
Striations & Chatter Marks Scratches and chips on bedrock that reveal the direction of ancient ice flow.
Moraines, Drumlins & Eskers The debris left behind — ridges, streamlined hills, and sinuous gravel trails of meltwater.
Icebergs & Calving Where glaciers meet the sea, they birth floating cathedrals of ice that drift, melt, and reshape coastlines.

🌍 Last Glacial Maximum

~26,500 – 19,000 years ago. Sea level ~120 m lower. Ice covered much of Canada, northern Europe, and Patagonia.

📈 Interglacials

We live in one now — the Holocene. Ice still holds ~70% of Earth’s freshwater, mostly in Antarctica and Greenland.
